Overview

PuPath is a mobile-first campus tour PWA for XJTLU SIP Campus. The current version integrates map navigation, cross-campus routing, identity-aware recommendations, themed and custom tours, check-ins, collectibles, badges, story cards, bilingual switching, and accessibility options in one browser-accessible experience.

This portfolio focuses on process rather than promotion. It records the design problem, research input, personas, user journey, key requirements, and the path from early concept to a working public demo.

Goal

Reduce first-time campus orientation anxiety and improve route clarity.

Platform

Mobile-first Progressive Web App, accessible through a public URL.

Playful Mechanism

Themed routes, custom tours, check-ins, collectibles, badges, and story cards.

Core Users

Students, visitors, and staff.

Motivation

Campus orientation at XJTLU is not only a navigation problem but also a confidence and decision-making problem. In real first-week and short-visit situations, many students and visitors can identify a destination name but still struggle with practical questions: where to start, which places should come first, and how to connect locations into a meaningful route. In practice, this often leads to repeated detours, delayed arrival, and higher uncertainty, especially when users are unfamiliar with campus building functions or time-constrained tasks such as registration, classroom transitions, and service-point visits [1].

Another persistent issue is information fragmentation. Useful campus knowledge exists across map tools, official web pages, signs, and peer recommendations, but these sources are rarely integrated into one coherent orientation flow. Conventional navigation tools are strong at location search and shortest-path routing, yet they typically do not provide scenario-based check-in routes, role-oriented route priorities, or sufficiently clear multilingual support for international university contexts like XJTLU [2][3]. As a result, users may find coordinates but still miss context, sequence, and confidence during movement.

PuPath is motivated by the need to bridge this gap through a lightweight, browser-accessible approach that combines practical wayfinding with contextual campus understanding and playful engagement. Instead of replacing professional map apps, it complements them with themed routes, POI story cues, check-ins, and badge feedback that can guide first-time orientation behavior. The overall goal is to make campus orientation more contextual, inclusive, and engaging while keeping the prototype easy to access through a public web URL and practical for course-scale deployment and evaluation [4].

Research

This research section focuses on the gap analysis and stakeholder definition for PuPath. It reviews academic research and commercial products to identify what existing solutions do well, what they miss, and how these insights inform PuPath's campus-specific, scenario-based, and playful tour experience.

The Gap: Research and Product Review

Instead of only summarizing previous work, we reviewed what existing academic research and commercial products did well and what they missed. This helped us identify the design opportunity for PuPath: a campus-specific guide that combines reliable wayfinding, scenario-based routes, inclusive support, and lightweight playful exploration.

Academic researchReference [5]

Landmark-based Wayfinding

Source

Yesiltepe, D., Dalton, R. C., & Ozbil Torun, A. (2021). Landmarks in wayfinding: a review of the existing literature. Cognitive Processing, 22, 369-410.

What it did well

  • Reviews why landmarks are important in real and virtual wayfinding tasks.
  • Explains landmark visibility and salience as key factors for spatial orientation.
  • Shows that recognizable cues help users navigate large-scale outdoor environments.

What it missed

  • Focuses mainly on wayfinding theory rather than campus-tour implementation.
  • Does not address playful exploration, check-ins, or personal records.
  • Does not explain how landmarks should connect to user identity or campus scenarios.

Implication for PuPath

PuPath should make POIs visually and contextually recognizable through photos, categories, detail cards, and route context, so users can connect campus names with real places.

Academic researchReference [6]

Mobile Campus Touring with AR and GPS

Source

Cai, S., Pei, L.-S., & Shi, P.-F. (2013). Mobile Campus Touring System based on AR and GPS: a Case Study of Campus Cultural Activity. International Conference on Computers in Education.

What it did well

  • Directly studies a mobile campus touring system for cultural campus activity.
  • Uses smartphone sensors such as GPS, camera, Wi-Fi, and digital compass.
  • Connects real campus locations with situated cultural learning and interaction.

What it missed

  • AR-based interaction can increase technical complexity and device dependency.
  • Focuses more on cultural learning than everyday wayfinding and route confidence.
  • Provides less detail on long-term records, badges, or lightweight repeated use.

Implication for PuPath

PuPath should keep the campus-tour idea but implement it in a lighter PWA form, combining location-based exploration with practical route actions instead of relying on heavy AR.

Academic researchReference [7]

User-Centered GPS Mobile Tour Design

Source

Clements, K. (2017). Designing a GPS Mobile Tour: A Lesson in User-Centered Design. International Journal of Designs for Learning, 8(2).

What it did well

  • Presents a GPS-guided smartphone tour through a user-centered design process.
  • Shows how mobile tours are affected by environment, technology, and user context.
  • Emphasizes testing and refining a guided tour experience around real user needs.

What it missed

  • Its case is not specifically designed for a university campus navigation problem.
  • It does not focus on POI density, scenario route diversity, or campus service points.
  • It gives limited attention to playful reward systems such as badges and treasures.

Implication for PuPath

PuPath should treat the tour as an iterative, user-tested experience: users should find places, choose routes, follow progress, and receive feedback in a coherent flow.

Academic researchReference [8]

Gamification, Progress Feedback, and Rewards

Source

Kim, J., & Castelli, D. M. (2021). Effects of Gamification on Behavioral Change in Education: A Meta-Analysis. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 18(7), 3550.

What it did well

  • Provides meta-analytic evidence about gamified interventions in education.
  • Shows that gamification can support behaviour change and learning outcomes.
  • Helps justify progress feedback, badges, and achievement moments as motivational tools.

What it missed

  • Gamification effects depend on context and may not automatically transfer to navigation.
  • It does not specify how badges should be explained in a campus tour interface.
  • It does not address the balance between utility-first navigation and playful rewards.

Implication for PuPath

PuPath should use lightweight playful feedback, but badge conditions and progress rules must be transparent so rewards support exploration instead of confusing users.

Academic researchReference [9]

Accessible Wayfinding and Navigation

Source

Prandi, C., Nisi, V., Salomoni, P., & Nunes, N. J. (2023). Accessible wayfinding and navigation: a systematic mapping study. Universal Access in the Information Society, 22, 185-212.

What it did well

  • Maps research on devices and software for accessible wayfinding and navigation.
  • Highlights barriers faced by people with disabilities and special needs.
  • Shows that both indoor and outdoor environments require inclusive navigation support.

What it missed

  • Does not focus specifically on playful campus tour experiences.
  • Accessible route data can be difficult to collect fully for a campus prototype.
  • Does not directly address visitor storytelling, badges, or discovery records.

Implication for PuPath

PuPath should make inclusive support visible through language settings, accessibility options, emergency contacts, and future route data for safer and more accessible campus movement.

Research Implications

The research shows that PuPath should not be designed as a normal map replacement. It should first solve reliable campus wayfinding, then support scenario-based route selection, inclusive access, and lightweight playful exploration.

1. Reliable Wayfinding First

The system must make campus places easier to identify through POI categories, photos, detail cards, and route actions.

2. Scenario Routes Matter

Users need route choices that match real campus goals, such as going to class, visiting landmarks, finding food, or moving across campus.

3. Playful Features Should Support Usefulness

Check-ins, stories, badges, and records should make exploration more memorable without distracting from navigation confidence.

From Pain Points to Design Response

Based on the pain points identified in the current journey, PuPath was designed as a campus-specific navigation and exploration tool rather than a generic map replacement. Scattered information is addressed through a single PWA entry point; unclear building recognition is supported by POI categories, photos, and detail cards; route uncertainty is reduced through scenario-based route cards and progress feedback; and safety concerns are supported through weather hints, emergency contacts, accessibility settings, and language options.

Evidence of Life

Ideation & Crazy Eights

In this ideation stage, we used rapid sketching to decide the UI visual direction and the layout of key functional blocks. Instead of only comparing abstract concepts, we focused on how users would enter the app, read map information, discover recommended content, and access personal progress with minimal cognitive load. The sketches helped the team test hierarchy, card grouping, navigation rhythm, and page-level consistency before moving into high-fidelity design.

The final direction combined a clear mobile hierarchy, map-driven wayfinding, and modular content blocks so that Home, Map, Recommendation, Detail, and My pages work as one coherent user flow.

Three Possible Ways the System Could Work

Each alternative focuses on a different primary interaction model. The comparison helped us decide whether PuPath should behave mainly like a map tool, a guided tour system, or a playful discovery platform.

Alternative 01

Map-first Campus Navigation

Users start from a campus-specific map, browse POIs, filter place categories, open place detail cards, and start navigation from a selected destination.

Best suited for

  • Finding classrooms, teaching buildings, parking areas, food places, and service points.
  • Connecting building names with real campus locations.
  • Supporting quick, practical wayfinding tasks.

Strengths

  • Highly practical and easy to understand.
  • Directly responds to the strongest navigation pain points.
  • Fits well with a mobile-first PWA prototype.

Limitations

  • May feel too similar to a normal map if used alone.
  • Playful value is limited unless route progress or discovery features are added.

Alternative 02

Scenario-based Guided Tour

Users select a route based on a real campus scenario, such as a freshman route, visitor route, food route, cross-campus route, or themed exploration route.

Best suited for

  • Helping users choose a suitable route without manually planning every stop.
  • Showing route duration, station count, current stop, next stop, and progress.
  • Supporting new students, visitors, and staff-guided campus reception.

Strengths

  • Turns campus movement into a structured journey.
  • Improves confidence through progress feedback and stop lists.
  • Connects well with check-in and lightweight achievement mechanics.

Limitations

  • Less flexible for users who only want a single destination quickly.
  • Requires carefully designed route categories to avoid overwhelming users.

Alternative 03

Playful Discovery and Memory System

Users explore campus through recommended content, story cards, check-ins, badges, treasures, favorites, visited records, and recent views.

Best suited for

  • Making campus places more meaningful than simple coordinates.
  • Encouraging repeated exploration and personal campus memory.
  • Supporting playful value through discovery, collection, and lightweight rewards.

Strengths

  • Creates the strongest playful and memorable experience.
  • Extends the journey beyond arrival.
  • Connects well with Discover content and personal records.

Limitations

  • Cannot solve urgent wayfinding problems by itself.
  • Should remain lightweight so it does not distract from core navigation tasks.

Selected Direction: Map-based Scenario Campus Tour with Playful Discovery

The final PuPath concept does not choose only one alternative. Instead, it combines the most useful parts of all three alternatives, while keeping the navigation task as the foundation.

Why this direction was selected

We selected a hybrid direction because the user research shows that PuPath must first solve practical campus wayfinding, but it also needs to provide a playful campus tour experience. Alternative 1 provides the necessary POI map and destination search foundation. Alternative 2 adds scenario-based route selection, stop lists, progress feedback, and check-in moments. Alternative 3 contributes discovery content, stories, badges, treasures, and personal records.

This combination allows PuPath to remain useful for quick navigation while also supporting staged progress, meaningful campus discovery, and continued exploration.

Evaluation

To evaluate whether PuPath effectively supports campus wayfinding and playful exploration, we conducted usability testing with the Alpha version of the system. The evaluation focused on whether users could find campus places, choose suitable routes, understand route progress, use playful features, and notice inclusive support functions.

What We Wanted to Test

The evaluation goals were aligned with the core and playful requirements defined earlier. The purpose was not only to check whether the interface worked, but also to understand whether the system improved user confidence and exploration experience.

1. Wayfinding Effectiveness

Test whether users can quickly find a target campus place through the POI map, category filters, and place detail page.

2. Route Clarity

Test whether users can choose a suitable scenario route and understand the current stop, next stop, and route progress.

3. Playful Engagement

Test whether users understand check-ins, badges, Discover content, and personal records as part of campus exploration.

4. Inclusive Support

Test whether users can find language settings, accessibility settings, weather hints, and emergency contact information when needed.

Usability Testing Setup

We tested the Alpha prototype with three target users. Each participant completed the same task sequence while we observed task completion, confusion points, comments, and post-test feedback.

Participant 1

User type: New student / campus-unfamiliar user
Reason: Represents users who need help finding buildings and understanding routes.

Participant 2

User type: Current student / frequent campus user
Reason: Represents users who value fast access to common places and efficient route choice.

Participant 3

User type: Visitor / international or staff-related user
Reason: Represents users who need clearer guidance, bilingual support, and safety information.

Task Design

The test tasks were designed to cover the main PuPath user flow: finding a place, checking details, choosing a route, following progress, using a playful action, and reviewing personal records.

  1. T1: Find a campus POI. Ask the participant to find a teaching building, food place, or service point on the map.
  2. T2: Open place details and start navigation. Ask the participant to open a POI detail card and find the action for navigation.
  3. T3: Choose a scenario route. Ask the participant to compare route cards and select a route that fits a given scenario.
  4. T4: Complete a check-in and read progress feedback. Ask the participant to confirm arrival at a stop and explain what changed in the route progress.
  5. T5: Save or review a personal record. Ask the participant to favorite a place, mark a place as visited, or find recent records in My Page.

What We Learned

The findings show which parts of the Alpha prototype already supported user needs and which parts required refinement before the final version.

Finding 1: POI density affected search efficiency

Users could understand the POI map, but when many markers were displayed together, it became harder to locate a specific destination quickly. The map needs clearer POI hierarchy, filtering, or clustering.

Finding 2: Route options were not diverse enough

Users understood the route card format, but the current route set felt relatively limited. More scenario-based routes are needed to support different tasks, weather conditions, accessibility needs, and visitor situations.

Finding 3: Badge feedback needed clearer rules

Users liked the idea of badges as exploration feedback, but the unlocking logic was not obvious. The system should explain badge conditions, progress, and reward moments more clearly.

Finding 4: Support functions need visibility

Language, accessibility, and emergency support were useful, but should remain easy to notice without interrupting the main navigation flow.

Finding 5: Personal records encouraged return use

Favorites, visited places, and recent views helped users understand that PuPath can support continued campus exploration beyond one visit.

Finding 6: Privacy explanation should be clearer

Because PuPath stores records locally, the interface should explain that user records are saved on the current device and can be cleared.

Reflection

This reflection summarizes what we learned from designing, implementing, and evaluating PuPath. The main lesson is that playful interaction is valuable only when it supports real campus wayfinding, user confidence, and inclusive access. Therefore, PuPath should not be treated as either a pure map tool or a pure gamified experience, but as a practical campus guide enhanced by lightweight playful exploration.

Project Reflection

The following reflections connect our design decisions, technical choices, ethical considerations, and AI-assisted development process.

Design reflection

Navigation First, Playfulness as Support

The project showed that PuPath should not be designed as a purely playful application. Users first needed reliable wayfinding, clear POI information, and understandable route choices. Playful features such as check-ins, badges, and personal records became meaningful only when they supported real campus movement.

What this means for PuPath

  • Map navigation and scenario routes should remain the foundation.
  • Playful feedback should help users understand progress, not distract from the task.
  • Badges and records need clear logic so users know why they receive feedback.
Social and ethical reflection

Inclusion, Independence, and Privacy

PuPath may improve campus inclusion by helping new students, visitors, international users, and staff-led guests explore the campus with less uncertainty. It can reduce the need to repeatedly ask others for directions and give users more independence.

Ethical considerations

  • Location, visited places, and exploration records should be treated as sensitive user data.
  • The current no-login and local-record approach reduces unnecessary data collection.
  • Future sharing or account features should provide clear consent, visibility control, and data deletion options.
Technical reflection

Prototype Architecture and Its Limits

The PWA architecture made PuPath lightweight and easy to access without installation or mandatory login. Local data and browser storage were suitable for the prototype stage because they allowed us to implement POIs, routes, records, badges, and preferences with low deployment complexity.

Limitations

  • Real campus navigation would require more accurate and maintained route data.
  • POI and accessibility information need continuous verification.
  • A production version may need a backend for content management, moderation, and updates.

Technical Reflection: AI-assisted Development

Because the project used AI-assisted coding, layout, and placeholder-writing support, this section explains what prompts were used, how AI-assisted outputs were verified against user requirements, and what ethical considerations were considered for AI-supported work.

Prompt usage

1. Actual Prompt Groups Used

The AI prompts were organized as staged coding logs rather than one-off questions. They supported the structure and implementation of the main PuPath modules, while the team supplied the real campus content:

  • Project bootstrap: Ask AI to read PRD / UI / development documents, create a 0-to-1 Vite development plan, split it into an executable task board, and guide GitHub Pages deployment.
  • Map and navigation: Ask AI to connect Home / Tours with Map navigation, separate route navigation from point-to-point navigation, implement progress / next stop / manual check-in states, switch to AMap, and improve cross-campus routing and mobile map controls.
  • POI and recommendation structure: Ask AI to create data schemas, placeholder entries, category structures, card templates, and linking logic. The actual POI names, images, campus details, and recommendation content were filled and checked by the team.
  • Check-in and treasure system: Ask AI to help refine the check-in flow, local validation logic, daily limits, failure states, Captain Bird / XJTLU Bear collectibles, and the connection between check-in progress and badge logic.
  • Accessibility and settings: Ask AI to create a persistent accessibility settings page with font size, high contrast, screen-reader assist, mobile-stable scaling, install guidance, and local-record reset.
  • Story card placeholders and copy structure: Ask AI to provide placeholder card formats, bilingual wording patterns, and readability suggestions. The actual story card messages and campus-specific content were written, selected, and verified by the team.
Verification

2. How We Verified AI-assisted Outputs

  • Checked whether generated features matched the original prompt constraints, such as Vite implementation, GitHub Pages deployment, P0-first development, AMap routing, POI overlay, and bilingual UI consistency.
  • Manually tested the main user flows: entering from Home / Tours, jumping to Map, route navigation, point-to-point navigation, progress update, next stop display, manual check-in, and ending navigation.
  • Compared AI-assisted POI and recommendation structures with our own campus data, including category completeness, ID-name consistency, real POI detail links, and image-asset mapping.
  • Conducted field testing for check-in and reward behaviour against the required states: inside / outside campus, already checked in, location unavailable, daily limit, collectible result, and badge unlock condition.
  • Reviewed accessibility settings across pages to ensure font scaling, high contrast, screen-reader assist, and local data reset remained stable on mobile.
  • Replaced AI placeholder content with team-filled project content and revised outputs when they included unsupported assumptions, broken layout, inconsistent bilingual copy, or functions that did not match the user requirements.
Ethical considerations

3. Ethical Considerations

  • AI-generated code and placeholder content were treated as drafts that needed human review, not as final proof that the system met user needs.
  • AI was not used to create fake user research, fake evaluation data, or unsupported human-centric justification.
  • Location-related features were reviewed carefully because check-ins, visited records, and navigation history may involve sensitive user behaviour.
  • Accessibility-related AI suggestions were verified manually so that font scaling, high contrast, and screen-reader support were not only described but also reflected in the interface.
  • AI placeholder content was checked for bilingual clarity and then replaced or revised with team-filled campus content to avoid misleading route, POI, or weather-dependent claims.
  • AI-assisted coding prompts were saved in the main PuPath project repository's AI logs folder; this portfolio references that record rather than duplicating the full logs.

Key Lessons Learned

The project helped us understand how to balance practical usefulness, playful engagement, inclusive access, and technical feasibility.

1. Playfulness needs a purpose

Check-ins, badges, and treasures should support campus understanding and route progress rather than becoming isolated decorative features.

2. Campus context matters

Generic maps are useful, but campus users need building meanings, service points, route purposes, and identity-based guidance.

3. Trust depends on clarity

Users need to understand where information comes from, how records are stored, and what actions such as check-ins or badges mean.
